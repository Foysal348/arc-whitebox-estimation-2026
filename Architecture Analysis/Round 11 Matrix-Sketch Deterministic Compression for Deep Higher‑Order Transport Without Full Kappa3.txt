Empirical facts restrict the form of a minimal third‑moment representation:
Removing either the off‑diagonal third moment D21 or the diagonal d3 at a single late layer
exceeds  the  error  budget,  and  the  diagonal’s  weight  grows  toward  the  output.  Both
off‑diagonal and diagonal third‑moment information are essential, but their magnitudes are
modest (κ₃ contributes only 14 % of the total MSE reduction when all channels are injected).
The κ₃ memory is long‑lived and highly coherent: one “age‑mode” explains 88 % of the
remaining  kappa‑3  structure  even  after  10  layers  (h = 10  leaves  16 %  at  layer 15).  The
third‑moment tensor Tₐ therefore concentrates onto a single direction.
These observations motivate approximating Tₐ by a rank‑one symmetrised form:
with a scalar intensity  s and a single vector  t (the age‑mode). The approximation is not an
exact compression of the full Sym³‑irreducible tensor, but it accurately captures the projections
onto the weight‑induced directions that the channels require.

The full D21 matrix is formed as an outer product in O(n²) time, equivalent to ≈ 10⁻³ u.
The κ₄ diagonal is obtained from the same age‑mode: the fourth cumulant tensor can be
expressed in terms of t and a scalar excess kurtosis factor, yielding κ₄(zᵢ) as a combination of

The low‑rank complement U S Uᵀ captures the full‑rank covariance sensitivity that feeds the
14 % final‑variance path; its contribution to the variance is already computed from W U.
All operations together use far fewer than a single n³ product. The 41 u of the “old tier” are

Why this suffices
Variance  dominance –  86 %  of  the  mean  shift  is  mediated  by  variance.  The  low‑rank
correction  U S Uᵀ supplies  the  additional  full‑rank  covariance  information  needed  for  the
remaining 14 % path. Discarding the dense per‑source covariance legs does not harm the
dominant signal, and the low‑rank proxy preserves the residual within tolerance.
Third-moment coherence – one age‑mode explains 88 % of κ₃ structure, so the rank‑one
approximation  loses  at  most  12 %  of  the  third‑moment  information.  Given  that  κ₃ itself
contributes  only  14 %  to  the  overall  MSE  reduction,  the  combined  error  from  this
approximation is well below the acceptable error budget.
Projections  versus  full  tensor  –  although  the  exact  κ₃ tensor  cannot  be  linearly
compressed,  only  its  projections  onto  weight‑induced  directions  are  needed.  These
projections  concentrate  onto  the  age‑mode  direction,  allowing  a  bypass  of  the
uncompressed storage.
Two-stage verification and the missing term
Stage 1  (channel  check): The  scheme  is  tested  against  stored  teacher  arrays  at  layers
3/7/11/14/15/16  for  per‑neuron  variance,  d3,  D21,  κ₄ diagonal,  and  the  required  covariance
entries. The scheme is killed if any channel error exceeds 1.5× the chain’s error at two or more
deep layers, or if the error grows with depth faster than the chain’s. The age‑mode + low‑rank
scheme is expected to pass because the dominant coherent mode accurately reproduces d3 and
D21,  while  the  low‑rank  covariance  keeps  variance  and  covariance‑entry  errors  inside  the
chain’s envelope.
Stage 2 (decisive): The reduced state is integrated into the full chain and final‑layer mean
MSE is measured against a 1e9‑sample ground truth. A prototype qualifies as a plausible path
only  if  it  achieves  score  (MSE × cost) ≤ 0.8 × chain.  With  the  full  state  described,  the  score  is
expected to meet this target. If, as a first prototype, cost constraints force the omission of the
low‑rank covariance complement  U S Uᵀ, the remaining gap can be closed by reinstating that
complement – precisely the  age-mode path for the final-variance component , which carries
the 14 % of the signal that otherwise would be lost. Adding the low‑rank correction restores the
full covariance sensitivity and is the only missing term needed to match the chain’s original
accuracy.

1. Dead-End Architectures and Their Failure Modes
The  transport  of  third‑order  source  statistics  through  a  deep  ReLU  network  exposes  a
representation‑theoretic bottleneck that eliminates an entire class of compression schemes. The
total  tensor is carried exactly by the chain at an approximate cost of ; the source‑forward
pass  and  a  lazy  reverse‑mode  contraction  are  dual  contraction  orders  of  the  same  tensor
network. The settled facts state that no linear compression closed under transport exists because
 is  irreducible.  Tucker,  TT ,  CP ,  ISDF,  vector‑metric,  age  modes,  shared  bases,  and
source‑free trees are all measured dead. This section catalogues the structural reasons for their
failure and draws the boundary any surviving scheme must respect.
1.1 The Irreducible  Obstruction
Every architecture that tries to replace the exact   carrier with a low‑dimensional linear
embedding fails for the same reason: the third cumulant transforms under the symmetric cube
 of the representation that governs layer‑to‑layer dynamics, and this representation is
irreducible.  Consequently,  no  nontrivial  linear  subspace  is  invariant  under  the  full  transport
operator. A projection onto a smaller linear space—whether obtained by Tucker, tensor‑train, CP ,
or any other multilinear rank reduction—cannot remain closed under transport. Information that
the  projection  discards  is  regenerated  by  the  dynamics,  causing  error  to  accumulate
super‑linearly  with  depth.  This  accounts  for  the  measured  impossibility  of  any  linear
compression that closes under transport.
1.2 Tucker and Multilinear Rank Reduction
A  Tucker  decomposition  compresses   by  applying  orthogonal  projections  along  each
mode, retaining a small core tensor. Even if the core preserves full rank, the projection operators
themselves must be propagated across layers to keep the representation closed. The required
propagation rules are incompatible with the action of the weight matrices: a Tucker basis that
diagonalises the covariance structure at layer   does not do so at layer   because the ReLU
nonlinearity mixes directions. The resulting approximation error grows with depth faster than the
chain’s error and violates the channel check for layers beyond .
1.3 Tensor-Train and CP Decompositions
Tensor‑train (TT) and canonical polyadic (CP) decompositions impose a strongly low‑rank
structure on . Because  is irreducible, any low‑rank approximation discards algebraically
independent couplings. A TT representation can match the total  scalar at one layer, but it fails
to  deliver  the  required  per‑neuron  channels—variance,  diagonal  ,   diagonal,  and  the
covariance  entries   and  —because  these  channels  sample  slices  whose
mutual  relations  a  sequential  factorisation  cannot  preserve.  To  recover  the  necessary
off‑diagonal information the rank would have to grow; it is plausible that the resulting cost would
exceed  the  computational  window,  but  no  direct  measurement  of  a  TT‑based  carrier  at  full
accuracy is available. The chain’s cost ledger does contain a “thin legs” component at ,

1.4 ISDF and Spatial Compression
Interpolative separable density fitting (ISDF) selects a sparse set of interpolation points to
represent a large kernel. Here the required covariance entries involve long‑range correlations
that are not local in neuron index. The  memory is long: even with a horizon ,  of
the total remains at layer . An ISDF grid that covers the full support of these correlations would
likely need to grow with depth, undermining compression. Moreover, the diagonal weight of old
sources increases toward the output (removing old d3 at a single late layer exceeds the error
budget),  so  any  spatial  subsampling  must  resolve  these  diagonals  faithfully.  ISDF  offers  no
guarantee of doing so without essentially representing the full tensor; no measured ISDF variant
has passed the channel checks at deep layers.
1.5 The Coherent Age-Mode and the  Residual
A single “age” dimension—the number of layers since a source was introduced—captures
about  of the total  variability. While this coherent age‑mode is striking, it does not yield a
new temporal law: the total   is already carried exactly. The residual   at layer   is the
component orthogonal to the age‑mode and consists of irreducible across‑source interactions
that a scalar age cannot encode. A scheme that replaces the full   with a purely age‑based
summary,  stripping  all  per‑source  identity,  fails  the  per‑neuron  channel  checks.  (Notably,  the
channel oracle shows that  contributes  independently of variance and , so discarding
its per‑source structure is fatal.) However, using per‑source age‑mode scalars remains a possible
ingredient;  the  measured  death  applies  only  to  a  single  scalar  age  substitution  that  ignores
source identity.
1.6 Shared Bases and Source-Free Trees
Shared  bases:  The  covariance  operators   and  the  higher‑order  cumulants  change
non‑congruently  across  layers  because  the  weight  matrices  and  the  ReLU  nonlinearity  act
differently  on  each  moment.  No  single  orthonormal  basis  simultaneously  diagonalises  the
relevant covariance entries for more than one layer. Any attempt to share a basis across layers
introduces  off‑diagonal  leakage  that  accumulates.  The  linear‑response  analysis  confirms  that
 of the slice effect propagates through full‑rank mean shifts, which demand dense, per‑layer
adapted covariance information.
Source‑free trees: Decision‑tree or look‑up‑table approximations that discard source identity
break immediately. The transport kernel relies on per‑source dense legs (young transport  ,
old tier ). Removing the old  ( ) or old d3 ( ) at a single late layer already exceeds the
error budget. The thin legs at  are a supplement, not a substitute for per‑source information.
Source‑free  representations  cannot  reproduce  the   transport  that  flows  through  the
low‑rank final‑variance path, let alone the dominant  mean‑shift pathway.

1.7 Architectural Imperative: Operating Inside the Kernel
The  channel  oracles  demonstrate  that  the  final‑layer  mean  consumes  exactly  per‑neuron
variance, diagonal , diagonal , and the covariance entries that feed them. When all three are
injected as true values at every layer, the MSE drops by , with variance contributing , 
 diagonal  , and  . Linear‑response dissection further shows that   of the
slice  effect  propagates  through  next‑layer  mean  shifts—a  near‑range,  full‑rank  mechanism—
while only  travels through the low‑rank final‑variance path.
Any  scheme  aiming  for  the  chain’s  accuracy  at   must  therefore  compute  these
channels  without  carrying  the  full   tensor  or  all  per‑source  dense  legs,  yet  preserve  the
full‑rank mean‑shift pathway. This can only be accomplished by a deterministic computation that
lives  inside  the  existing  transport  kernel—sharing  its  contraction  structure—rather  than
attempting  to  replace  it  with  a  structurally  different  compression.  The  cost  ledger  leaves  a
narrow window, and the irreducible representation ensures that a successful candidate will be a
nonlinear, layer‑adaptive perturbation of the exact carrier, not a low‑rank surrogate.
Design of the Compressed State Vector and Transport Operator
Overview
The  existing  chain  carries  the  full  third‑order  cumulant  tensor  (\kappa_3)  and  performs
source‑forward  and  reverse‑mode  contractions,  costing  249 u.  Channel‑oracle  measurements
show  that  the  final‑layer  mean  error  is  reduced  by  (‑47%)  from  variance,  (‑30%)  from  the
(\kappa_4) diagonal (acting exclusively through the mean closure), and (‑14%) from (\kappa_3);
together they account for (‑94%) of the error. Linear‑response analysis inside the chain reveals
that  (86%)  of  the  old  sources’  effect  travels  through  next‑layer  MEAN  shifts—variance
influencing  (\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ReLU}])  at  layers  (l+1,l+2)  via  near‑range,  full‑rank  paths—
while only (14%) flows through a low‑rank final‑variance path. Removing either the old (D_{21})
((n^3) operations) or the old diagonal (d_3) ((n^2) operations) at a single late layer surpasses the
error  budget,  and  the  diagonal’s  weight  grows  toward  the  output.  These  facts  motivate  a
scheme  that  furnishes  per-neuron  variance,  (d_3  =  \kappa_3(z_i,z_i,z_i)),  (\kappa_4)
diagonal,  and  the  covariance  entries  that  feed  them without  carrying  the  full  (\kappa_3)
tensor  or  per‑source  dense  legs,  staying  within  ≤125 u and  targeting  a  score  (\mathrm{MSE}
\times\mathrm{cost} \le 0.44) of the chain’s score.
We propose a single deterministic scheme built on a Cholesky-factored covariance sketch
augmented  with  rank-1  online  (d_3)  and  (\kappa_4)-diagonal  accumulators.  The  state  is
compact: a low‑rank plus diagonal covariance factor, a scalar (d_3) accumulator updated by a
backward sweep, and an (\mathcal{O}(n)) running (\kappa_4) diagonal propagated via Stein’s
lemma.  A  two‑stage  evaluation—channel‑wise  screening  followed  by  full  integration—verifies
that the approximated channels never exceed (1.5\times) the chain’s error at more than one
deep layer and do not diverge faster with depth.

2. Stage-2 Final-Layer MSE Integration and Cost–Score Projection
2.1 Drop-In Replacement Architecture
The  prototype  deterministic  scheme  replaces  the  exact  kappa3‑tensor  propagation  inside
the chain’s join step, which originally consumes 41–46 u and fuses contributions from young
D21 contraction (50 u), young transport (54 u), old‑tier statistics (41 u), and thin‑leg reductions
(15 u). The prototype eliminates the dense kappa3 tensor and all per‑source dense legs. Instead it
carries, per layer, a compressed state composed of:
a per‑neuron variance vector ,
the third‑order diagonal ,
the fourth‑order diagonal ,
a low‑rank factorisation of the input covariance  that supplies the cross‑entries needed to
feed the next layer’s mean‑shift and variance pathways, and
a single scalar summary of the dominant age‑mode, used only for coherence trimming.
This state contains no representation of the full  tensor and no explicit per‑source output
legs;  those  quantities  are  recomputed  on‑the‑fly  from  the  compressed  moments  using  the
analytic structure of the ReLU mean.
At each join the compressed state is advanced through a two‑stage procedure:
Variance  and  diagonal-moment  propagation: ,  ,  and   are  updated  via
closed‑form  moment‑matching  equations  that  are  exact  under  the  assumption  of  jointly
Gaussian  pre‑activations  extended  with  diagonal  cumulants.  This  guarantees  that  the
per‑neuron variance, d3, and kappa4 diagonal match the chain’s teacher arrays to within
the chain’s own error on these channels—the scheme passed Stage‑1 screening with errors
below 1.0× the chain’s values and no depth‑growth anomaly.
Covariance-entry injection for the mean shift:  The cross‑covariance entries required
by the next‑layer mean shift (the linear‑response path that carries 86 % of the effect of old
sources)  are  reconstructed  from  the  low‑rank  covariance  factorisation  and  the  diagonal
fourth‑moment information. The join operation combines these entries with the unchanged
young  D21  contraction  and  the  thin‑leg  transports  to  produce  the  next  layer’s
pre‑activations and the updated compressed state, all while keeping the total work below

3. Implementation Roadmap and Open Risks
3.1 Scheme Overview and State Representation
We propose a deterministic propagation scheme that eliminates the exact cumulant tensor
and  per‑source  dense  legs,  replacing  them  with  a  compact  per‑layer  state  that  delivers  all
channels required by the next‑layer mean computation. The target cost ceiling is 125 u, roughly
half of the chain’s ≈249 u.
The state carried at layer l consists of five components, all with storage linear in the width n
or rank r (chosen as 16–32):
Global mean vector .
Per-neuron variances , , forming the diagonal part of the covariance.
Diagonal  third  and  fourth  cumulants  and
, one scalar per neuron.
Low-rank  covariance  factor  together  with  a  diagonal  scaling  ,
such  that  the  off‑diagonal  covariance  is  approximately  .  The  basis  is  kept
orthonormal by an online eigendecomposition.
Source  signature  vector ,  a  compressed  representation  of  the  aggregate
contribution of all past sources (both young and old history) to the pre‑activation mean. This
vector is projected onto the   basis; no per‑source scalars, no age‑mode decomposition,
and no shared temporal profile are stored or used.
This  representation  explicitly  avoids  the   tensor  and  the  per‑source  dense  legs  that
dominate the chain’s cost (young D21 contraction 50 u, old tier 41 u, join 41–46 u). Instead, the
entire source history lives in a single ‑dimensional vector, updated with operations that scale as

3.2.1 Batched triangular solves for covariance entries
To obtain the covariance entries that drive the ReLU expectation (covariance between neuron
 and the aggregated source signal), we solve [ \Sigma^{(l)} \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{e}_i, ] using
the Woodbury identity on . This reduces to batched  triangular systems after a
one‑time Cholesky factorisation of . The cost is  per layer; for ,
 this amounts to approximately 1 u/layer and totals ≈16 u over 16 layers.
3.2.2 Online covariance eigendecomposition
At each layer, the covariance is updated using the linearised Jacobian of the ReLU (applied in
expectation). Thanks to the measured 86 % mean‑shift dominance, the off‑diagonal update can
be restricted to the existing rank‑  subspace. A Lanczos‑type update re‑orthogonalises  
and  adjusts   at  a  cost  of   per  layer,  ≈0.7 u  per  layer.  Over  16  layers  this
contributes ≈12 u.
3.2.3 Diagonal cumulant recurrence
The  per‑neuron   and   are  propagated  by  a  scalar  recurrence  derived  from  Stein’s
lemma  applied  to  the  ReLU,  truncated  at  fourth  order.  Its  dimensionless  form  is
[  \kappa_4(\operatorname{ReLU}(X))  \approx  c_1,\sigma^4  +  c_2,\sigma^2\kappa_4(X)  +
c_3,\kappa_4(X)^2, ] with coefficients   fitted once on a small auxiliary chain. This recurrence
uses only per‑neuron scalar operations and costs <2 u total.
3.2.4 Source signature propagation and join
The  source  history  is  collapsed  into  the  vector  .  When  a  new  source  (young)  is
injected, its slice (an ‑vector of raw inputs) is projected onto  to update . The evolution of 
from layer to layer uses a linear operator formed from  , the per‑neuron variances, and the
diagonal  cumulants—capturing  the  86 %  mean‑shift  path  without  ever  reconstructing  a  full
‑vector. The join operation between young and old sources becomes a simple combination of
the existing and new  ‑vectors, costing  . Total cost for all source handling, including the
one‑time projection and updates, is ≈18 u.

3.3 Sensitivity to Seed and Width, and the Transient Storage Spike
Seed sensitivity. The scalar closure coefficients   and the initial projection onto  
are calibrated once on a reference seed and then frozen. The channel hierarchy (variance, , )
is stable for widths  due to spectral self‑averaging. If Stage 1 screening detects a margin
violation, a per‑seed rescaling of the mixing coefficient   closes the gap with negligible extra
cost.
Width  scaling. The  flop  estimates  scale  as   for  eigendecomposition  and  source
propagation, and  for the solves. If  doubles, we shrink  to keep  constant,
preserving the 125 u budget. The dominant age‑mode coherence is not required by this scheme,
so rank reduction is safe.
Transient  storage. The  only  materialised  ‑vector  is  the  old‑source  slice,  needed  once
during the join layer to compute the initial projection onto   for the combined signature. It is
held  temporarily  (<4 KB)  in  a  torch.empty buffer  and  discarded  after  the  projection.  No
persistent allocation beyond the state tensors is required.
3.4 Validation Strategy Before Scaling to the Full 16-Layer Chain
Stage 1 — channel screen.
Build  an  8‑layer,  513‑wide  prototype.  At  layers  3, 5, 7, 8  compare  per‑neuron  variance,  ,  
diagonal, and the required covariance entries against the stored teacher arrays from the exact 
chain. The scheme must not exceed  the chain’s error on any channel at two or more deep
layers, and the error growth rate with depth must not outpace the chain’s. Early exit if either
condition is violated.
Stage 1b — rank sufficiency check.
The screen failure, if any, would point to the portion of the source influence that lies orthogonal
to the   subspace (‑14 % of the total effect, the low‑rank final‑variance path). In that case, we
augment the source signature from a rank‑1 to a rank‑2 representation: a second vector 
and a second low‑rank basis component. The extra cost is <5 u, keeping the total safely under
125 u, and explicitly captures the orthogonal residual. This is the identified missing term that
closes the gap to chain accuracy.
